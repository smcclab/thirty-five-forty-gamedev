## Recap: brainstorming

Brainstorming is a formalised system of idea generation, and it is a skill
that takes practice. State a challenge, do not criticise, vary the method,
work somewhere playful, put it on the wall, go for lots of ideas, and stop
before you burn out (Fullerton p.171--174).

Three methods are used in this workshop:

- **List creation** -- list everything on a topic, then variations.
- **Mind map** -- start with a core idea and radiate others out.
- **Stream of consciousness** -- write anything that comes to mind.

### Narrowing the list

Once you have plenty of ideas (Fullerton p.182--188):

- Shortlist five to ten and discuss each thoroughly. Consider the merits of
  each, stay positive, compare strengths. Narrow to three, and schedule a new
  session to expand those.
- In second-level brainstorming, focus in on features and define the "X" of
  the game -- its creative centre -- as a **razor** and a **slogan**. The
  razor cuts: it determines which features belong and which do not. The slogan
  is catchy: it tells players whether this sounds like something they want to
  do.
- When the key features and the X are clear, write one or two paragraphs
  capturing the basic gameplay, and hold an informal feedback group. Does the
  idea appeal to players? If not, it is still cheap to change.

## Activities

### Activity 1: Brainstorming your proposal game (20 min)

Brainstorm individually for 20 minutes.

Your challenge, for the [Game Proposal and Prototype](/assessments/proposal/):
design a single-player experience for the Unity games engine that can be
played in 15 minutes or less and runs on Unity Play. Start from, or build on,
the player experience goal you set in Week 2.

Choose one of the three methods above and work on paper or on a device.

### Activity 2: Brainstorm with a partner (20 min)

Working in pairs, 10 minutes each way.

- Explain your idea to your partner, using your chosen brainstorming method.
- What ideas do they have to build on yours?
- What new ideas do *you* have, from explaining your idea or hearing theirs?
- Keep it going: write (list, stream) or draw (map) as you generate ideas.

Then swap.

### Activity 3: Describe your game idea (10 min)

Working individually, write down:

- What is the essence of your game? (the razor)
- What makes your game interesting? (the slogan)
- What is your basic gameplay?

This is Fullerton's exercise 6.7 (p.188).

### Activity 4: Pitch your game idea (30 min)

Working in your table group, go around the table and pitch your idea --
roughly five minutes each.

- Are others interested in or excited by your idea?
- Do they have feedback or suggestions?
- Were any new ideas generated by the discussion?

Then write down your reflections: what useful feedback or suggestions did you
get, and what did you learn about your game idea?

### Activity 5: Turn your idea into a game (20 min)

Formal elements form the structure of a game (Fullerton ch. 2, p.33--38 and
p.50, and ch. 3). Working individually, write down the answers you can. You do
not need to have all of them yet -- this is the beginning.

| Element | Questions |
|---|---|
| Players | How many? Requirements? Roles? |
| Objectives | What is the objective of the game? |
| Procedures | What are the required actions for play? |
| Rules | Any limits on player actions? Rules for behaviour? |
| Resources | What objects are valuable to players? Why? |
| Conflict | What causes conflict in the game? |
| Boundaries | What are they? Physical or conceptual? |
| Outcome | What are the potential outcomes? |
