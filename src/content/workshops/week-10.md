## Recap: playtesting

Playtesting is something the designer performs throughout the design process
to gain insight into how players experience the game. There are many ways to
do it, from the informal and qualitative (ten friends in your garage) to the
structured and quantitative (hundreds of people in a professional lab). The
goal is the same: useful feedback from players to improve your game, and
confidence that it is functioning as intended, internally complete, balanced
and fun to play. (Fullerton ch. 9, p.277--310.)

The designer is the player's advocate throughout development. Playtesting is a
continual iterative process whose cycles get tighter as production moves
forward: smaller and smaller issues, smaller and smaller changes, and no
fundamental changes towards the end. Playtesting only in beta, with a fully
functional game, is too late -- you are stuck with the core gameplay. Playtest
and iterate from the start.

### Conducting a session

Your role is investigator and observer, not designer. You give playtesters
access to the game, lead them through a useful playtest, record what they do
and say, and analyse their responses. Do not tell them what to think about it
or explain how it works. Let them play with minimal explanation and let them
make mistakes; see how each person approaches it. Ideally an objective person
runs the test while you watch from a distance.

A test script keeps you on track and reminds you of your role. Its structure:

| Section | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 2--3 min | Welcome the playtesters, thank them, introduce yourself and anyone else present, briefly explain the process, and say if you are recording (and that it is for your reference only). |
| Warm-up discussion | 5 min | Questions to get them talking: what games do you play? what do you like about them? what was the last game you bought? |
| Play session | 15--20 min | Explain the game is in development, that you want their feedback, and that you are testing the game rather than their skill. Then either leave them alone and watch remotely, or watch quietly from behind. |
| Discussion | 15--20 min | One-on-one, probing overall appeal, interest, challenge and understanding. |
| Wrap-up | -- | Thank them. A small token gift is a nice touch. |

**Think aloud.** Either way you observe, ask playtesters to think aloud while
playing: what are they thinking, choosing, confused about? You learn far more
than from watching alone. They will forget, so remind them and ask questions
-- "what are you trying to do now?" It feels strange at first, so encourage
them and let them practise.

**Discussion questions.** Examples:

- Overall, what were your thoughts about the game?
- What were your thoughts about the gameplay?
- Were you able to learn quickly how to play?
- What is the objective of the game?
- How would you describe the game to someone else?
- Is there anything you wish you knew before starting to play?
- Is there anything you didn't like about the game?
- Was there anything you found confusing?

You will have your own questions based on your goals. Focus on the most
important ones for this stage.

Schell's "FFWWDD" set (p.482--495) is another way in:

- What was the most **f**rustrating moment or aspect?
- What was your **f**avourite moment or aspect?
- Was there anything you **w**anted to do that you couldn't?
- If you had a magic **w**and to change, add or remove anything, what would it
  be?
- What were you **d**oing in the experience?
- How would you **d**escribe this game to your friends and family?

### Tips

- Listen to feedback without responding or defending. Write down criticisms;
  don't argue with them.
- You are looking for things to improve, not for praise. It can be hard to
  hear criticism of your creation, but it is better to hear the bad news now
  than after release.
- Do not lead your playtesters to say what you want to hear.
- People in groups sway each other, and some will dominate.

### Taking notes

You cannot remember all the feedback, and what you do remember will be what
you wanted or expected to hear. Write down the date, the feedback from testers
and your own observations. Use a form alongside your script, with three parts:

1. **In-game observations** -- your thoughts as you watch them play.
2. **Post-game questions** -- to elicit opinions on key aspects.
3. **Revision ideas** -- your ideas for making the game better.

Not all questions will be relevant; tailor them. Write down more questions
than you plan to use and rank them by importance, but don't overwhelm the
playtesters -- go for quality and focus.

## Activities

### Activity 1: Scrum (15 min)

Run a scrum meeting for your team. Each person reports what they did since the
last meeting, what they will do before the next one, and what problems they
are facing.

### Activity 2: Playtesting priorities and plan (15 min)

In your team, plan the first playtesting session for your game, to be
conducted in next week's workshop with your current working build. This is
your first chance to get feedback from representatives of your target audience
-- the classmates who will be evaluating your game.

- What are your playtesting goals for this session? What is the most valuable
  feedback you can gather at this point? What are the most important questions
  to answer?
- How much support will players need to be able to play your game at this
  stage? Will you have training in the build? Does the game have a start and
  an end?

### Activity 3: Write a playtesting script (45 min)

In your team, write the script for next week's session. This is a *script*:
write down exactly what you will say, and read it out to the testers.

- **Introduction (3--5 min):** introduce yourself, give an overview of the
  process, get the game ready, and explain only the absolute essentials about
  the game -- you mostly want to watch them work it out. Say that you want
  their feedback, and ask them to think aloud as they play.
- **Play session (15 min):** watch and take notes. Write down any questions
  they ask, but don't answer them -- ask what they think instead. Remind them
  to think aloud if they stop.
- **Discussion (5--10 min):** a set of guiding questions, open and not
  leading, to get the feedback you need.
- **Wrap-up:** thank them for testing your game.

### Activity 4: Team review and reflection (30 min)

As in [Week 8](/workshops/week-08/), review your goals and priorities,
schedule and sprints, and communication plan, and adjust them if necessary.

- Have your project goals and priorities changed?
- Are you on track against your schedule and sprints?
- Is your communication plan working?
- Is everyone contributing?

Then reflect on the issues and opportunities raised: what is working, what
isn't, where you could improve, and what you need to change. If you are
experiencing issues your team cannot resolve, ask your tutor about a
moderation session.

## After the workshop

- Work on your sprint towards a playable version.
- **Publish a playable version to Unity Play.**
- Bring devices you can playtest on -- ideally one per team member.
- Finalise your playtesting script and bring it to the next workshop. Print it
  if you can; otherwise you will need a second device to read it from, since
  the first will be running the game.
