The course turns from individual work to the [Game
Project](/assessments/project/). You meet your team, demo the prototypes
everyone built before the break, choose one concept (or something new) to
develop, and leave with a prioritised feature list, a sprint schedule and a
communication plan.

From this week on, workshops are run in your project team.

## Recap: the Game Project

Teams of four to five, formed during the teaching break, build a playable game
in Unity. You can choose your team from within your own workshop; otherwise
you are allocated one. You must have submitted your proposal to be eligible to
join a team.

The restrictions are:

- **Single-player only** -- playable by one player.
- **Computer game** -- playable on a PC or Mac with a keyboard and mouse.
- **Unity Play** -- published as a WebGL game.
- **15 minutes** -- your game will be played by peers for exactly 15 minutes.

The submission has three parts: the game itself, published to Unity Play; a
coversheet completed and signed by every group member; and the peer
assessment. The coversheet and peer assessment carry no marks of their own,
but the game scores zero if either is missing or late.

Free assets from the Unity Asset Store and other approved sources may be used
and must be declared and acknowledged both in your Unity files and on your
coversheet. Paid assets must not be used without the convener's approval.

### How your peers will mark it

Each statement is rated 1--5, from strongly disagree to strongly agree, and
markers also give positive feedback and constructive criticism.

| Theme | Statements |
|---|---|
| Playability and clarity | The game was playable; the goals of the game were clear; I understood how to play; I knew how to win or complete the game; the controls were easy to use; the interface was clear; I knew my status or score; I was given feedback on my actions |
| Learning | It was easy to learn how to play; it was fun to learn how to play; I did not feel confused; I did not feel frustrated |
| Engagement | I wanted to win or complete the game; the game offered interesting choices; the level of challenge was right for me; the pacing was right for me; the gameplay was interesting |
| Overall | The game was original or innovative; I enjoyed playing the game; I wanted to keep playing the game |

Keep this rubric in front of you when you choose a concept and set priorities.

## Recap: agile game development

Agile is popular with game developers because it suits the problem: you do not
know exactly what you are creating. It is a philosophy, not a prescribed
methodology. The elements game developers use (Schell p.103--105):

- **Flexible goals** -- not merely tolerating changes to the plan, but
  planning for them.
- **Prioritised backlog** -- a list of features sorted by priority; things are
  added and removed, and it is reprioritised each sprint.
- **Sprints** -- a few weeks long, with a concrete working deliverable at the
  end.
- **Scrum meetings** -- short (10--15 minute) stand-up meetings where each
  person reports what they did yesterday, what they will do today, and what
  problems they face. Solutions are discussed after the meeting.
- **Demo day** -- everyone gets together at the end of a sprint to see what
  the team produced, creating a new baseline.
- **Retrospectives** -- a meeting at the end of a sprint to reflect on the
  process: what is going right, what is going wrong, what to adjust.

Fullerton (p.431--440) describes the same process as adaptive and
people-centric: address priority features to set short-term goals, work
closely together to implement them in sprints, meet daily or weekly to
evaluate progress, set new goals and identify blockers.

### Agile project planning

A project plan is a feature-based schedule and budget.

- **Goals** -- gameplay goals (mechanics, levels), technical goals, target
  platform, proposed launch date.
- **Priorities** -- organise goals by priority. The core mechanic is highest.
  What can you not launch without?
- **Schedule** -- time-bound iterations, usually two to four weeks, each
  focused on implementing prioritised features into a working build. The
  overall schedule is your estimate of the time to implement everything.

## Activities

### Activity 1: Meet your team (10 min)

Even if you think you know each other, everyone introduces themselves:

- What is your preferred name?
- What program are you enrolled in?
- Do you have any particular experience or interest in game development?
- What are your key strengths to contribute to the project?
- What are you most hoping to learn or improve through this project?

### Activity 2: Present your game prototypes (15 min)

Take turns to present your prototypes to each other. Show your team your
working prototype and explain the proposal idea as you demonstrate it. You do
not need to choose one yet.

### Activity 3: Choose a game concept (25 min)

Discuss the concepts your team presented and decide what you will develop.
Keep the project restrictions and the marking criteria in mind.

- How excited are you about each concept?
- What are the strengths of each? What are the risks?
- Does the concept conform to the restrictions, or can it be made to?
- What has been done, and what is left to do to complete it?
- Do you have the time and skills to turn the concept into a playable and
  enjoyable game that will do well against the criteria?
- What kind of experience will a player get in 15 minutes of the final game?

You do not have to pick one of the proposed concepts. You can make something
else, or combine several ideas -- but consider the risk of starting fresh.

### Activity 4: Identify your goals and priorities (20 min)

Discuss your project goals and the features that need to be implemented.

- What are the goals of the project, given the restrictions and the marking
  criteria?
- List the features that need to be implemented: gameplay, interface,
  technical, content, anything else.
- Prioritise the list. What is essential? What can you not release without?
  What is next most important? What is merely nice to have?

This prioritised list is the foundation of your backlog.

### Activity 5: Plan your schedule and sprints (15 min)

Develop an overall schedule covering all your features and sprints up to the
release date. You have a little over five weeks -- it is not long.

- How long will your sprints be, and how many will you have? One to two weeks
  each is a reasonable size.
- What will you aim to achieve in each? You should have a working game at the
  end of every sprint: keep it playable.
- Who is responsible for what? Can you assign any tasks now?

A suggested shape is two weeks, two weeks, one week, then final polish. Bear
in mind that playtests are run in the workshops in
[Week 11](/workshops/week-11/) and [Week 12](/workshops/week-12/), using
your most recent working version, and that the overall schedule will change as
you reprioritise after each sprint.

### Activity 6: Team communication plan (10 min)

Discuss how you will keep in touch and manage the project.

- What communication channels will you use?
- Will you use any project management software?
- How often will you meet to discuss progress?
- Will you plan to work together in person sometimes?
- Do you have each other's contact details?
- Who will set up and share the team's version control repository?
- What are your expectations of each other?

## After the workshop

- Work on your first sprint towards a playable version.
- Follow your communication plan.
- Review the [Developer Diary](/assessments/diary/) requirements and keep a
  log of your work.
- Start your first diary entry, and bring it to the next workshop.
