The first workshop of the course. You work in table groups on a shared
brainstorming exercise, learn what the formal elements ask of a design, build
and break a paper prototype of Battleships, and finish by brainstorming the
player experience goal for your own [Game Proposal and
Prototype](/assessments/proposal/).

## Before the workshop

- Attend the orientation lecture.
- Review the Week 1 and Week 2 theory material.
- Start working through the Unity Junior Programmer Pathway:
  - Week 1 projects: Getting Started, Unit 1 -- Player Control
  - Week 2 project: Unit 2 -- Basic Gameplay

Bring paper and pens. Most of this workshop happens away from a screen.

## Recap: brainstorming

Brainstorming is a formalised system of idea generation. It is a skill, and it
takes practice. Doing it with other people is more stimulating and more
productive than doing it alone.

Best practice (Fullerton p.171--174):

- **State a challenge.** Set a specific challenge for the session.
- **No criticism.** Do not self-edit, and do not criticise or ignore others.
  You want free thinking and ideas built on other ideas. "Yes, and" helps.
- **Vary the method.** Different structures suit different people.
- **Playful environment.** Leave your desk, bring some toys.
- **Put it on the wall.** Get up; talk, think, sketch, write.
- **Go for lots of ideas.** Try for a hundred.
- **Don't go too long.** You need a break after about 60 minutes.

Example challenges -- design a game:

- "in which players must make strong alliances and then betray them"
  (experience goal)
- "with a special role for parents to play together with their children"
  (audience focused)
- "that makes interesting use of only one button for control"
  (technology driven)

### Alternative methods

Fullerton p.174--181 lists several:

- **List creation** -- list everything on a topic, then variations.
- **Idea cards** -- single ideas or words on index cards; mix them up, draw
  two to four, generate ideas on the combinations.
- **Mind map** -- start with a core idea and radiate others out, using lines
  and different coloured markers to connect them.
- **Stream of consciousness** -- write anything that comes to mind, without
  self-editing or worrying about style; read it back after ten minutes.
- **Shout it out** -- record yourself for five minutes, then listen back and
  transcribe.
- **Cut it up** -- random words and images from print media, mixed and
  matched.
- **Surrealist games** -- more formal games such as Exquisite Corpse. Write an
  article and an adjective on a sheet of paper, fold it and pass it to your
  neighbour, who writes a noun; fold and pass; a verb; fold and pass; an
  article and adjective; then read the poem aloud. ("The exquisite corpse
  shall drink the new wine.")
- **Research** -- a subject of interest rather than randomness, including
  physical experience of it (a game about fishing, say).

## Activities

### Activity 1: Blue-sky brainstorming (25 min)

Working in your table group, use **mind mapping** to brainstorm a "game
controller" for one of these characters -- or another of your own choice:

- busy parent
- superhero
- politician
- university student
- pet

Start with the core concept in the centre and map verbs, actions and the
feelings associated with them around it. Use lines and different coloured
markers to connect ideas.

About the character:

- What does the character do?
- What makes the character interesting?
- What aspect of the character would it be engaging to control?
- How does the character react?
- Does the character have free will?

Features for your controller:

- What will it look like?
- What could each button do?

### Activity 2: Describe your game idea (5 min)

Still in your group, discuss:

- What is the essence of your game? (the **razor**)
- What makes it interesting? (the **slogan**)
- What is your basic gameplay?

The razor *cuts* -- it lets the team decide which features belong and which do
not. The slogan is catchy -- it lets players decide whether this sounds like
something they want to do. *Medal of Honor*'s razor was "GoldenEye set in WWII
on a PlayStation"; its slogan was "Prepare for your finest hour."
(Fullerton p.182--188.)

### Activity 3: Turn your idea into a game (20 min)

Formal elements form the structure of a game (Fullerton ch. 2, p.33--38 and
p.50, and ch. 3). In your group, answer what you can for your idea:

| Element | Questions |
|---|---|
| Players | How many? Requirements? Roles? |
| Objectives | What is the objective of the game? |
| Procedures | What are the required actions for play? |
| Rules | Any limits on player actions? Rules for behaviour? |
| Resources | What objects are valuable to players? Why? |
| Conflict | What causes conflict in the game? |
| Boundaries | What are they? Physical or conceptual? |
| Outcome | What are the potential outcomes? |

Then spend five minutes discussing the exercise itself:

- Was it easy to define the formal elements? What was easy, what was hard,
  what was missing?
- What else would you need to do to define them properly? More brainstorming?
  Refining the idea? Prototyping? Playtesting?

### Activity 4: Prototyping Battleships (20 min)

Physical prototypes are easy to construct, easy to change and easy to throw
away. They use paper, cardboard, household objects and drawings; they need no
special expertise; and they focus attention on gameplay rather than
technology. Building and revising them instils a deep understanding of game
principles -- how to build a structure for a game, how elements interact, and
how the game functions as a system. (Fullerton p.203--206.)

Working in pairs, build and play Battleships (15 min):

1. Take one grid sheet each. Outline two 10x10 grids and label the cells A--J
   and 1--10 on each. Label one grid **Opponent Ships** and the other
   **My Ships**.
2. Draw these ships on your My Ships grid. They cannot be placed diagonally,
   and you hide your grid from your partner.
   - Aircraft carrier (1 x 5 cells) -- AAAAA
   - Battleship (1 x 4 cells) -- BBBB
   - Cruiser (1 x 3 cells) -- CCC
   - Submarine (1 x 3 cells) -- SSS
   - Destroyer (1 x 2 cells) -- DD
3. Take turns to attack by calling out a grid position. Track hits and misses
   against your opponent on your Opponent Ships grid, and hits on your own
   ships on your My Ships grid.
4. Sink a ship by hitting all its cells, and say so when a ship sinks. The
   first player to sink all the opponent's ships wins.

Then discuss with your table (5 min) how it functions *as a prototype*:

- Does it accurately represent the game mechanics?
- Does the "artwork" provide enough of an experience for someone to grasp the
  game and give feedback?
- What would happen if you made the grid bigger?
- How could you test changes to the gameplay in your prototype? What other
  changes could you test?
- How would you prototype *Tetris*, or another simple digital puzzle game?
- What about a more complex digital game -- an action game, a shooter, a
  strategy game?

### Activity 5: Brainstorm your player experience goals (20 min)

Player experience goals are the first way to bring the player into the design.
They are goals for the *type of experience* the player will have, not for
features, and they say nothing about how they will be implemented. Features
are brainstormed later to meet the goals, and then playtested to see whether
the goals are actually met. (Fullerton p.12.)

Examples:

- "Players will have to cooperate to win, but the game will be structured so
  that they can never trust each other."
- "Players will have the freedom to pursue the goals of the game in any order
  they choose."
- "Players will feel a sense of happiness and playfulness rather than
  competitiveness."

Working individually, brainstorm the player experience goal for the prototype
you will build in Unity for the [Game Proposal and
Prototype](/assessments/proposal/). The constraint is a single-player game a
player can experience in under 15 minutes. Say what the player will feel or
experience, not what the game's features are.

Pick a method -- list creation, mind mapping or stream of consciousness -- and
work on paper or on a device for 20 minutes.

:::tip
Photograph or save whatever you produce. Evidence of your brainstorming is
part of the [Game Prototype Progress](/assessments/progress/) submission.
:::
