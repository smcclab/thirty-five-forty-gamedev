This is the build week. You write down your core gameplay, diagram the game as
a system of objects, properties, behaviours and relationships, and then make a
physical prototype on the table -- first its foundation, then its structure --
playing each version yourself and with a partner.

- Continue the Unity Junior Programmer Pathway: Unit 3 -- Sound and Effects,
  then Unit 4 -- Gameplay Mechanics.
- Review the Week 2 and Week 4 theory.

Bring paper, pens and anything you can use as tokens. You will be building a
prototype by hand.

## Recap: core gameplay

Start with the **core gameplay mechanic** -- the actions a player repeats most
often while striving to achieve the game's overall goal. Games are repetitive
by nature: the core actions stay the same and are built upon over the course
of the game. Diagramming those core actions can expose problems early, such as
feedback loops. (Fullerton p.216--217.)

Examples:

- *Monopoly*: players buy and improve properties with the goal of charging
  rent to other players who land on them in the course of play.
- *Super Mario Bros.*: a player controls Mario, making him walk, run and jump,
  while avoiding traps, overcoming obstacles and gathering treasure.
- *WarCraft III*: players build and move units on a map in real time with the
  intent of engaging opposing units in combat and destroying them.
- *Diablo III*: players battle monsters, seek treasure and explore dungeons in
  an attempt to amass wealth and become more powerful.

## Recap: games as systems

A game is a set of formal elements which, set in motion, create a dynamic
experience the player engages with. Systems can be simple or complex,
predictable or unpredictable. (Fullerton p.129--133.)

| Element | What it is |
|---|---|
| Objects | The basic building blocks -- physical or abstract; pieces, concepts, players, areas, terrain. Defined by their properties, behaviours and relationships. |
| Properties | Qualities that define physical or conceptual aspects of an object: colour, location, health, strength, level, experience. |
| Behaviours | Potential actions an object might perform in a given state: moving, fighting, using items. More behaviours means less predictability, and more complex is not always better. |
| Relationships | The connections between objects, central to any system. Without them objects are only a collection. Based on location, hierarchy, sequence, changes in the game or player choices, and determined by chance or by rule sets. |

## Recap: building the physical prototype

Fullerton's four stages are **foundation**, **structure**, **formal details**
and **refinement**. This workshop covers the first two; you do the other two
in your own time.

**1. Foundation** (Fullerton p.217--218) -- build a representation of the core
gameplay. The goal is to design the basic game objects (the physical setting,
units, resources) and the key procedures (the repetitive action cycles that
keep the game in motion). Draw a board layout or map and use arts and crafts
to make pieces. Questions will arise as you go -- how many squares may the
player move? how do they interact with objects? how is conflict resolved? --
and you put them aside for later. Play it yourself: does the concept work?
Avoid adding new rules unless absolutely necessary, and keep the core gameplay
to as few rules as possible.

**2. Structure** (Fullerton p.231) -- construct a framework to support the
full feature set. Prioritise what is most essential. Decide which *rules* are
essential and which are *features* the structure must support.

- **Features** are attributes that make a game richer: weapons, vehicles, new
  ways to navigate the space.
- **Rules** are modifications to game mechanics that change how the game
  functions: winning conditions, conflict resolution, turn order.

You can add rules without adding features, but not the other way around: one
new feature might add ten or more new rules. Focus on rules first and features
later. Rules tend to be linked to the core gameplay; features tend to be
peripheral.

## Activities

### Activity 1: Describe your core gameplay (20 min)

Working individually (10 min): using the game description you wrote in
[Week 3](/workshops/week-03/), write a description of your core gameplay. What
are the actions a player repeats most often while striving to achieve your
game's overall goal?

In your table group (10 min): take turns to read your core gameplay aloud. For
each person, give feedback on whether it

- captures the core gameplay -- the repeated actions and the overall goal, and
- sounds like a fun or interesting game you would like to play.

Note any feedback you receive, for the next iteration.

### Activity 2: Draw your core game system (10 min)

Working individually, draw or map your core game system:

- the core **objects** and their **properties**
- the **behaviours** of each object
- the **relationships** between objects

### Activity 3: Prototyping your game foundation (40 min)

Working individually (30 min), build a representation of your core gameplay.
The goal is to design the basic game objects and key procedures.

- Draw a board layout or a map.
- Use paper, pens and tokens to make pieces.
- Add the basic objects and key procedures.
- Try playing it on your own. Does the concept work? What questions arise as
  you build and play it?

Working in pairs (10 min), play each other's games. Explain how yours works,
then let your partner try it. Does it work? Does it make sense? Is the core
concept fun?

### Activity 4: Prototyping your game structure (20 min)

Working individually (10 min), extend the prototype to support the full
feature set.

- What are the essential *rules* of your game, as distinct from features? For
  example movement or action rules, a scoring system, health.
- Add one or two essential rules.
- Play it on your own again. Does it still work? What questions arise?

Working in **new** pairs (10 min), play each other's games again, as in
Activity 3.

:::tip
Photograph your prototype after each round. Images of your physical and
digital prototypes are the evidence for the prototyping-process section of
the [Game Proposal and Prototype](/assessments/proposal/), and for [Game
Prototype Progress](/assessments/progress/).
:::

## The two stages you do at home

**3. Formal details** (Fullerton p.231--232) -- add rules and procedures to
make a fully functional game. Use the formal elements to decide what the game
needs. Is the objective interesting and achievable? Is the player interaction
structure the right choice? Are there rules or procedures you want that are
not part of the core mechanic? The challenge is the right level of detail; new
designers usually add too much. Aim for a small, important set of features
that all contribute to your experience goal. Add new rules one at a time and
test. If the game cannot function without it, leave it in; if you can build
the game without it, leave it out.

**4. Refinement** (Fullerton p.232--233) -- test in a controlled environment,
over a number of iterations, to create a compelling game. Now is the time for
non-essential ideas, but not all at once. Rank your features by necessity;
introduce and test one at a time, then remove it. Some great ideas will
diminish playability, and some that seemed dull will add a new dimension.
Write down an analysis after testing each one, and incorporate your
playtesters' feedback.
