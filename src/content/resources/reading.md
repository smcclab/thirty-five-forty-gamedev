## Set texts

The [theory decks](/lectures/) cite two books by page throughout. You will get
much more out of the course with access to both.

- Tracy Fullerton. 2019. *Game Design Workshop: A Playcentric Approach to
  Creating Innovative Games*, 4th edition. CRC Press, Taylor and Francis Group.
  The course structure follows this book: the playcentric process, formal
  elements, prototyping, systems, balance and playtesting all come from it, and
  its exercises are the ancestors of several workshop activities.
- Jesse Schell. 2020. *The Art of Game Design: A Book of Lenses*, 3rd edition.
  CRC Press, Taylor and Francis Group. A complementary set of design "lenses"
  --- short prompts that make you look at your game from a particular angle.
  Useful when you are stuck.

A third book is recommended for background:

- Katie Salen and Eric Zimmerman. 2003. *Rules of Play: Game Design
  Fundamentals*. MIT Press.

## Foundational works on play and games

These are the sources behind the week 1 definitions of play and games, and are
worth reading if you want the argument rather than the summary.

- Johan Huizinga. 1938. *Homo Ludens: A Study of the Play-Element in Culture*.
  The origin of the "magic circle" idea: play as an activity set apart from
  ordinary life by its own place and rules.
- Roger Caillois. 1961. *Man, Play, and Games*, translated by Meyer Barash. The
  Free Press of Glencoe. Sorts play into competition, chance, simulation and
  vertigo, along an axis from free improvisation to rule-bound structure.
- Bernard Suits. 1978. *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia*. The
  much-quoted definition of playing a game as the voluntary attempt to overcome
  unnecessary obstacles.
- Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi. 1990. *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience*.
  The source of the flow model used in week 3 to talk about the balance between
  challenge and skill.
