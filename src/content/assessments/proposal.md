## Purpose

The Game Proposal and Prototype asks you to demonstrate your developing
mastery of Unity and of game design concepts: a prototype that shows the game
working, and a proposal that analyses your design process and the game design
theory behind your decisions.

## Restrictions

The same restrictions as the [Game Prototype Progress](/assessments/progress/)
item apply:

- **Single-player only.** Design the game to be played by one player.
- **Computer game.** Design it to be played on a computer (PC or Mac) with a
  keyboard and mouse.
- **Unity Play.** The prototype must be published as a WebGL build to Unity
  Play.
- **Primitives only.** No assets are permitted, including free, paid and
  self-made assets. Materials may be used; textures may not. Focus on
  gameplay.

:::warning
A 50% penalty applies to the prototype mark if assets or textures are used
(in 2024, -50 of the 100 prototype marks).
:::

## Prototype (10%)

Publish your prototype to Unity Play as a WebGL build, named
`ANU2024 <UID>` with your own UID and the current year. Unity's guide to
[creating and publishing WebGL builds](https://learn.unity.com/tutorial/creating-and-publishing-webgl-builds)
covers the process.

:::warning
Any change to your Unity Play game after the due date counts as a late
submission and attracts late penalties.
:::

This prototype is a **more developed version** of the early prototype you
submitted for the [Progress](/assessments/progress/) item. You should have
built the ideas out further so that the core gameplay is demonstrated. It is
fine if the concept has changed since the first version.

### How the prototype is developed

In weeks 1 to 5 you are expected to complete Units 1 to 5 of the Unity Junior
Programmer Pathway. The same labs underpin the prototype as for the Progress
item:

| Pathway lab | What it gives your prototype |
| --- | --- |
| Lab 2 -- New Project with Primitives | Background plane, primitive player, camera chosen for your game type, primitive enemies, obstacles, projectiles and materials |
| Lab 3 -- Player Control | A player controller with basic movement from player input, with appropriate constraints |
| Lab 4 -- Basic Gameplay | Basic gameplay: object movement, destruction, collisions and spawning |

The [workshops](/workshops/) develop the concept the prototype represents:

| Workshop | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| [Week 2](/workshops/week-02/) | Player experience goals |
| [Week 3](/workshops/week-03/) | Game idea, formal elements |
| [Week 4](/workshops/week-04/) | Core gameplay and system, game foundation and structure |
| [Week 5](/workshops/week-05/) | Interface design |

Your prototype should clearly be a digital representation of the unique game
concept you have been developing through the workshop activities.

## Proposal (21%)

Submit the proposal as a single Word document via Wattle, using the provided
template. The header table records your UID and name, your game's name on
Unity Play, its link, your preferred browser, and any instructions your marker
needs in order to play the prototype.

All word limits are maximums. Write only what you need, include the total word
count where the template asks for it, and expect your marker to stop reading
at the limit.

The proposal has two parts.

### Part 1: Game Design Analysis (15%)

Write this about the **full game concept**, not only about what the prototype
currently implements.

| Section | Limit | What to write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Core Gameplay | 100 words | The core gameplay: the actions a player repeats most often while striving to achieve the game's overall goal. Not play instructions -- the essence of the game. |
| Formal Elements | 800 words for the whole table | A table row for each of Players, Objectives, Procedures, Rules, Resources, Conflict, Boundaries and Outcome, with a Description and an Explanation of why the key decisions were made. All prototypes are single-player, so that does not need stating. No references needed in this section. |
| Engagement | 200 words | How you engage the player: play, challenge, choice, pleasure and so on. Specific examples with reference to the theory covered. |
| Mechanics | 200 words | The core mechanics and why; the system dynamics and information structures used; the key challenges in balancing the game and why. Specific examples with reference to the theory covered. |
| Interface | 200 words | The interface design choices you made and why, and how the interface supports your gameplay. Specific examples with reference to the theory covered. |

**References.** The Engagement, Mechanics and Interface sections must each
cite specific theory. References go in the template's reference table, one per
row, in [ACM referencing style](https://www.acm.org/publications/authors/reference-formatting).
Citing the course theory slides is acceptable, but you must reference a
specific slide -- for example `Penny Kyburz. 2024. Week X Theory Slides: Name
of deck.` Cite by number in the text, with a page where needed, as `[1, p.3]`.

### Part 2: Prototyping Process Analysis (6%)

| Section | Limit | What to write |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prototyping Process | 600 words | A table with one row each for week 5, week 6 and the teaching break, and columns for Activities and Reflections. Describe your workshop, Unity and personal prototyping activities briefly, and spend the words on the reflections: what changed through prototyping and what you discovered. Do not change the time periods. |
| Prototyping Evidence | -- | Numbered images of physical and digital prototypes, each referenced from the process table. No links; only evidence included in the document is looked at. |

This is the same instrument you used for weeks 1 to 4 in the
[Progress](/assessments/progress/) report, continued forward.

## Marking

In 2024 the prototype was marked out of 100, the Game Design Analysis out of
150 and the Prototyping Process Analysis out of 60.

### Prototype rubric (10%)

| Criterion | Weight | Marks | What the marker looks for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Game Interface | 2% | 20 | How complete, functional, appropriate and usable the camera, controls and user interface are for acting and interacting in the game. |
| Gameplay | 2% | 20 | How complete, functional, detailed and polished the gameplay is, including player and object movement, rules and behaviours. |
| Prototype | 2% | 20 | How effectively the prototype demonstrates the proposed game concept, and how well it maps to the prototyping work documented in the report. |
| Playable | 2% | 20 | How playable the prototype is, including clear instructions (in game or in the proposal document) and whether the core gameplay can be tested. |
| Originality | 2% | 20 | How much originality, creativity and flair the game shows, beyond being a clone of other games. |
| Asset penalty | -- | -50 | Primitives and materials only. Using assets or textures costs 50 marks. |

Each criterion's 20 marks are awarded in these bands:

| Standard | Marks | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exceptional | 20--16 | Exceptional against the criterion |
| Superior | 15--14 | Superior against the criterion |
| Good | 13--12 | Good against the criterion |
| Satisfactory | 11--9 | Satisfactory against the criterion |
| Incomplete | 8--0 | Incomplete, or lacking, against the criterion |

### Game Design Analysis rubric (15%)

| Criterion | Weight | Marks | What the marker looks for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Core Gameplay | 1% | 10 | How clear, detailed, concise and compelling the description of the core gameplay is. |
| Formal Elements | 8% | 80 (10 for each of the eight elements) | How clear, detailed, compelling and well justified the description and explanation of each formal element is. |
| Engagement | 2% | 20 | How well the approach to engaging the player is explained and justified, with specific examples and clear, appropriate references to theory. |
| Mechanics | 2% | 20 | How well the core mechanics decisions are explained and justified, including system dynamics, information structures and balancing challenges, with specific examples and references to theory. |
| Interface | 2% | 20 | How well the interface design decisions and their support for the gameplay are explained and justified, with specific examples and references to theory. |

Core Gameplay and each of the eight formal elements are marked out of 10:

| Standard | Marks | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exceptional | 10--8 | Exceptional description and explanation |
| Superior | 7 | Superior description and explanation |
| Good | 6 | Good description and explanation |
| Satisfactory | 5--4 | Satisfactory description and explanation |
| Incomplete | 3--0 | Insufficient attempt |

Engagement, Mechanics and Interface are marked out of 20, using the same
20-mark bands as the prototype criteria above.

### Prototyping Process Analysis rubric (6%)

| Criterion | Weight | Marks | What the marker looks for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Prototyping Process Activities | 3% | 30 (10 each for week 5, week 6 and the break) | Whether the activities are described clearly and concisely, each supported by a numbered piece of evidence that meaningfully backs it up, and whether they show an appropriate amount of prototyping effort. Time periods must not be changed; links are not followed. |
| Prototyping Process Reflections | 3% | 30 (10 each for week 5, week 6 and the break) | Whether the reflections -- changes made through prototyping, and what was learned -- are deep, insightful and reflect genuine learning and iteration. |

Each period's 10 marks use the same 10-mark bands as the Progress report:
10--8 exceptional, 7 superior, 6 good, 5--4 satisfactory, 3--0 insufficient.

### Standards and moderation

From the 2024 marking guide:

- The Game Design Analysis should be written about the full game concept, not
  just the prototype.
- Core gameplay should capture the repeated actions and the goal, not read as
  instructions for playing.
- Formal elements must describe *and* explain the design decision for each of
  the eight elements.
- Engagement, Mechanics and Interface should go into depth on a small number
  of examples rather than listing many. Referencing lecture slides is
  sufficient, but the reference must point at specific theory or a specific
  slide.
- The prototyping analysis must link activities to the game being developed,
  not just record course attendance. In 2024 the week 6 workshop was spent
  working on the proposal document, which is not itself a prototyping
  activity.
- Completing the work to a satisfactory standard is a pass, not a high
  distinction; markers were told to expect most students around the credit
  range, with roughly 20% in the HD range.
- Marking was budgeted at about 30 minutes per student, weighted towards the
  report rather than the prototype, with written comments on the prototype and
  the Game Design Analysis only.
