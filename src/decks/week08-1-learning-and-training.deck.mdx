## Play: why it matters

- Young animals are particularly playful
  - Anything in their environment is an excuse to start gameplay
  - Nobody teaches the animal this behaviour
- Play is fundamental to development and survival
  - Young animals refine the skills they need to hunt, fight, mate and hide as adults
- Play helps us learn, acquire knowledge, socialise, problem solve, relax, see things differently — “fun”

## Learning to play

- Good game design teaches the player how to play and enjoy the game
  - One of the most important concepts in game design
  - Not fun if the player does not know how to play
  - Awesome systems the player never exploits effectively do not exist
- From the smallest mechanic to whole levels, features and mechanics
  - Environment physics, player abilities, enemy behaviour, reward systems
  - The game as a whole suffers if these rules are not taught
- Designers must find the best teaching mechanisms for this training goal

## Mastery: the teaching dialogue

- What actions and skills must the player master to be rewarded?
- What are the most enjoyable sections to be found?
- Which weapons provide the best results?
- What creatures are most fun to play with?
- What skills should the player train first?
- These questions are a teaching dialogue between designer and player

## Player character: teach it early

- Teach this early in the gameplay experience
  - The sooner the player accepts it, the sooner disbelief is suspended
- Changes can come later, after the fundamental rules are taught
- Less central when play is not through an avatar
  - Players still need to know how they can manipulate the virtual world

## World physics and NPCs

- Teach players how to interact with the game’s environment
  - Actions mean little outside a defined physical context
- Teach players how to interact with friendly NPCs
  - Keeping them friendly may be a challenge itself
- Teach players about enemy AI
  - Abilities, hazards, strengths, weaknesses

## Success strategies

- A video game needs to be worth playing
- Player actions must be rewarding, especially when linked to progression
- Strategies exist that produce an enjoyable progression
  - Finding and perfecting them is part of the fun of the game
  - Central to learning how to enjoy the game

## Formal and informal strategies

- A success strategy is one that produces a fun gameplay experience
  - Formal — predefined solutions to gameplay questions
  - Informal — defined by the players themselves
  - Players devise their own play through open interaction with the world
  - Can be encouraged and supported through good design

<div class="image-credit">Kremers – Ch. 2 — Rouse – Ch. 7</div>

## Training approaches

- Recommended approaches
  - Teach by practical example
  - Positive reinforcement
  - Teach in safe environments
  - Prepare the player fairly
- Approaches to avoid
  - Don’t start with failure
  - Don’t taunt the player

## Practical example

- Learning by doing works best for many people
- Let players practise what they were taught as soon as possible
  - A new mechanic: let the player try it immediately
  - Easier while the lesson is fresh

## Positive reinforcement

- Actively reward players when they pass a skill test
  - Or when they progress through a challenging scenario
- Taught that success has positive consequences, players become eager to engage

## Safe environment

- Teach important skills in a safe environment first
- Players must be able to learn comfortably
- Ramp up difficulty later with harder encounters
  - Lessons at the start of the game should be forgiving

## Fairness

- Give the player the tools and knowledge before a test they can fail
- Lethal encounters are fine, but they must be fair
  - Avoid unannounced or unavoidable instant-death traps
- Introduce new mechanics in non-frustrating ways
- Teach skills in a way that players cannot fail
  - Let them practise until they can do the task

<div class="image-credit">Kremers – Ch. 2 — Rouse – Ch. 7</div>

## Don’t start with failure

- Tempting to start with a very serious challenge
- Beginning on a tense note puts the player in grave peril
  - Sounds good on paper, but a game is not a movie or book
  - Serious danger means a serious chance the player fails
  - Frustrating to fail at the very start of the game or level
- Off-putting rather than enjoyably tense — the game looks frustrating
  - Discourages the player from playing

## Don’t taunt the player

- A teaching section must end positively, however long mastery takes
  - Struggling players need encouraging in a positive manner
  - Don’t punish them for not mastering a mechanic or challenge
- Don’t shut off award paths or use taunting language
  - It puts off exactly the players who are struggling
  - They have the most to gain from an encouraging approach
  - They might get fed up and stop playing completely

## Training methods

- Many games used to rely on manuals
- Then they started using tutorials/help-systems
- Players don’t want to engage in “educational” experiences that don’t feel like playing the game
  - Learning != education
- Gradual progression combined with in-game support is the key
  - Spread learning out across the game – part of the fun
  - The player should always feel like they are playing

## Tutorials and progression

- Tutorials and in-game training are interactive learning experiences
  - Introduce the player to mechanics, controls and concepts
  - Good training is enjoyable and invisible to the player
  - Use sparingly — don’t over-explain, give it in small bites
  - Never a fall-back for bad design
- Gameplay needs to be introduced gradually
  - Too slow and players are bored; too fast and they are confused
  - Getting the progression right is key to enjoyment of the game

## Design to avoid training

- Use industry standards
- Meet player expectations as much as possible
- Let players customise controls
- Don’t over-complicate mechanics or controls
- Keep game concepts simple and sensible
  - If you can’t explain it, it’s too complicated

## Controls and interface

- Players must control and understand the game effortlessly
  - Controls and interface become invisible if designed well
  - Poor ones create barriers to immersion
  - Frustrating to know what you want your character to do and not do it
- Keep controls as simple and minimal as possible, but sufficient
- Developers get accustomed to bad or unique controls
- Allow multiple ways to perform the same action
- Also see Week 5 theory

## Output and feedback

- Condense a huge amount of data effectively
- Give as much information as possible “in-game”
  - Position, condition — animation, texture — weapons, spells in effect
- Use visual and audio, rather than text
- Use the GUI or HUD for the rest — score, lives, status
- Notify the player of important events
- Players need the status and outcome of their actions — feedback
- Also see Week 5 theory

## Training summary

- A direct and useful link between game design and learning to play
- Game design is a vehicle for teaching play and enjoyment
- Gameplay and game design function as a teaching mechanism
- Training applies to almost every aspect of gameplay
- Training should be practical, positive, safe and fair
- Training should be gradual and integrated with progression
- Design your game to minimise the training required
