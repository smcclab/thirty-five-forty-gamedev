## Theory: Learning & Training Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

  - What is play? Playful animals (Week 1 Theory recap)
- Play comes naturally to animals of a certain level of intelligence (e.g., pets)
- Easy to start playing a game based on simple, understandable rules
  - Catch the ball, retrieve the stick, my hand is prey, obstacle course
- Young animals are particularly playful
  - Almost anything in their environment is an excuse to initiate gameplay
  - Without anyone teaching the animal how to engage in this behaviour
- Play is fundamental to development and survival
  - Young animals refine skills that are necessary to hunt, fight, mate, hide and many other activities that are key to survival as an adult
- Play helps us learn skills, acquire knowledge, socialise, problem solve, relax, see things differently, "fun"

## Learning to Play

- Good game design teaches the player how to play and enjoy the game
  - One of the most important concepts in game design
  - If the game design does not support learning through training, it will never be fully enjoyed by the player
    - The game cannot be fun if the player does not know how to play
    - If underlying game systems are awesome, but not harnessed/exploited by the player, they effectively do not exist
- Can relate to the smallest gameplay mechanic up to the largest span of levels, features, or mechanics - physical rules of the environment, abilities of the player character, behaviour of enemies, game reward systems
  - The game as a whole suffers if these rules are not taught to the player
  - Teaching the player how to deal with these game systems forms a solid basis for a good gameplay experience
- Designers must try to find the best techniques available (teaching mechanisms) to achieve this training goal

## Mastery

- Good design is not just teaching the player the rules, but also allowing the player to use those rules in a way that is rewarding and fun
  - Much of the fun comes to the surface when the player is tested
- We must show the player how the game’s play mechanics tie in with the game’s reward mechanisms
  - What actions and skills does the player have to master in order to get rewarded by the game?
  - What are the most enjoyable sections to be found?
  - Which weapons provide the best results?
  - What creatures are most fun to play with?
  - What skills should the player train first?
- These questions present a teaching dialogue between designer and player

## Learning to Play

- Key areas to be taught:
  - Game’s main goals and rules
  - Abilities and limitations of the player character
  - Physics and scope of the gameplay world
  - Abilities and limitations of the non-player characters
  - Success strategies available to the player
- Goals and Rules
- Player needs to learn what constitutes the core gameplay experience
- They need to know early on what the game’s main objectives are and how to achieve them (level by level and overall)

## Learning to Play

- Player Character
- Provides the player with much of their interface to the game world
  - Player experiences and interacts with the world through their player character
  - Defines the player’s abilities and limitations in the context of the game’s reality
- Very important to teach this early on in the gameplay experience
  - The earlier this is accepted by the player, the earlier they can suspend their disbelief in the artificial construct of the game

## Learning to Play

- Physics and Scope of World
- Teach players how to interact with the game’s environment
- Gives further context in which to perform actions
  - Player actions by themselves don’t have much meaning unless they are performed within a defined physical context
- Non-Player Characters (NPCs)
- Teach players how to interact with friendly NPCs
  - Keeping them friendly may be a challenge itself
- Teach players about enemy AI
  - Abilities, Hazards, Strengths, Weaknesses

## Learning to Play

- Success Strategies
- A video game needs to be worth playing
- The actions a player takes in the game need to include gameplay that is somehow rewarding to the player, especially if linked to progression
- There are strategies available to the player that result in an enjoyable progression
  - Finding and perfecting them is part of the fun of the game
  - Central to learning how to enjoy the game
- A success strategy is a strategy that produces a fun gameplay experience
  - Can be formal – requiring predefined solutions to gameplay questions
  - Can be informal – defined by the player themselves
    - When the player can devise their own enjoyable gameplay through open interaction with the game’s environment
    - Can be encouraged and supported through good design

## Training Approaches

- Practical Example
- Learning by doing works best for many people
- Give players a chance to put things they have been taught into practice as soon as possible
  - When a new gameplay mechanic is introduced, let the player try it out immediately
  - It is easier to put lessons into practice when they are still fresh in the mind of the player

### Positive Reinforcement

- Make sure players are actively rewarded when they pass a skill test or successfully progress through a challenging gameplay scenario
- When players are taught that there are positive consequences for successfully navigating the game, they will become eager to engage with it

## Training Approaches


### Safe Environment

- Teach important skills in a safe environment first
- Make sure players can comfortably learn skills they need to learn
- Ramp up difficulty later on and provide more difficult encounters
  - Lessons at the start of the game should be forgiving

### Fairness

- Ensure the player has the right tools and knowledge before they are forced to perform a skill test that they can fail
  - Lethal encounters are ok, but it is always necessary to make them fair
  - Unannounced or unavoidable instant-death traps should be avoided
  - Introduce player to new mechanics in non-frustrating ways
  - Teach skills in a way that players cannot fail
    - Allow them to focus on practising the skill until they can perform the tasks relevant to the teaching exercise

## Training Approaches


### Don't start with failure

- It can be tempting to start with a very serious challenge
- Want to begin gameplay on a tense note and immediately put the player in grave peril
  - Sounds good on paper, but not a movie or book
  - If there is serious danger, there is a serious chance the player will fail the gameplay challenge
  - Frustrating for players who don’t pass this test and end up failing at the start of the game/level
  - Rather than being enjoyably tense, the experience is likely to be off-putting and make the player think the game will be frustrating – discourages the player from playing

## Training Approaches


### Don't taunt the player

- If a game section is about teaching, make sure the results are positive, even if the player takes a long time to master the skill
  - If players are struggling to pick up a skill, they need to be encouraged in a positive manner
  - Don’t punish them for not being able to master a mechanic or meet a gameplay challenge
- Don’t shut off award paths, use taunting language or employ any similar tactics, as you will put off the players who are struggling
  - End up punishing the players who have the most to gain from an encouraging approach
  - They might get fed up and stop playing the game completely

## Training Methods

- Many games used to rely on manuals
- Then they started using tutorials/help-systems
- Players don’t want to engage in “educational” experiences that don’t feel like playing the game
  - Learning != education
- Gradual progression combined with in-game support is the key
  - Spread learning out across the game – part of the fun
  - The player should always feel like they are playing

## Training Methods

- Tutorials / in-game training
  - Interactive learning experiences
  - Introduce the player to mechanics/controls/concepts
  - Good training is enjoyable and invisible to the player
  - Use training/tutorials sparingly, don’t over-explain/complicate
  - Give info to the player in small, manageable bites
  - Don’t use as a fall-back for bad design
- Progression
  - Gameplay needs to be introduced gradually
  - If too slow – players will be bored
  - If too fast – players will be confused and frustrated
  - Getting the progression right is key to enjoyment of the game

## Good Design Practices

- Design the game to avoid training
  - Use industry standards
  - Meet the player’s expectations as much as possible
  - Allow players to customise controls
  - Don’t over-complicate game mechanics/controls
  - Keep game concepts simple and sensible
    - If you can’t explain it, it’s too complicated

## Good Design Practices

- Controls and Interface (also see Week 5 Theory)
  - Players need to control/understand the game effortlessly
  - Interface/controls become invisible if designed well
  - Poor controls and interfaces create barriers to immersion
  - Very frustrating to know what you want your character to do but not be able to do it
  - Should be kept as simple and as minimal as possible, while providing sufficient control
  - Developers get accustomed to bad/unique controls
  - Allow multiple ways to perform the same action

## Good Design Practices

- Output/feedback (also see Week 5 Theory)
  - Must condense huge amount of data effectively
  - Give as much info as possible “in-game”
    - Position, condition (e.g., animation, texture), weapons, spells in effect
  - Use visual/audio, rather than text
  - Use GUI/HUD to display the rest (score, lives, status)
  - Notify player of important events
  - Player needs to know status / outcome of actions (feedback)

## Training Summary

- Direct and useful link between game design and the learning aspects of gameplay
- Game design can be seen as a vehicle to teach players how to play and enjoy the game
- Gameplay and game design function as a teaching mechanism
- Training techniques and strategies apply to almost every aspect of gameplay
- Training should be practical, positive, safe, and fair
- Training should be gradual and integrated with progression
- Design your game to minimise training required
