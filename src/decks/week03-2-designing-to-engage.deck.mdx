## Designing for Challenge

- Reaching and exceeding goals
  - One goal at the end, or subgoals along the way?
  - Are your goals too hard or easy to reach?
  - Are they clearly defined? Or hidden from view?
- Competing against opponents
  - What opportunities are there to create competition in your game?
  - What is driving player competition?
- Stretching personal limits
  - Accomplishment from setting own goals and achieving them
  - Can you incorporate this? Does it suit your players?
- Exercising difficult skills
  - Are you presenting opportunities to learn new skills? Can players master and display these skills?
- Making interesting choices
  - Are you providing choices with consequences? How can you improve the choices?

## Designing for Play

- Living out fantasies
  - What aspirations/fantasies does your game fulfil?
- Social interaction
  - Is your game making the most of potential social interactions?
- Exploration and discovery
  - Is exploration in your game exciting? Are you guiding the player, but not directing?
- Collection
  - People love collecting and completing collections
- Stimulation
  - Stimulating the senses – sight, sound, touch, movement
- Self-expression and performance
  - Giving people a chance to show off who they are
- Construction/Destruction
  - Feeling investment (building) or freedom (destroying)

## Improving Player Choices

- Player choice is one of the most important aspects of fun
- What makes a choice interesting versus not?
- How can you design choices that are more interesting?
- Consequence is key – the choice must alter the course of the game
- Decisions must have potential upsides and downsides
  - Risk versus Reward
- Inconsequential / minor decisions should be made to matter more or be eliminated
- Not all decisions should be life and death either
  - Decisions should rise and fall as the game progresses
    - Mirrors the dramatic arc

- Life and death
- Critical
- Direct and immediate impact
- Important
- Indirect or delayed impact
- Necessary
- Small impact, direct or indirect
- Minor
- No impact on outcome
- Inconsequential

## Improving Player Choices

- Types of decisions
  - Hollow: no real consequences
  - Obvious: no real decision
  - Uninformed: an arbitrary choice
  - Informed: the player has ample information
  - Dramatic: taps into player's emotional state
  - Weighted: balanced, consequences on both sides
  - Immediate: has an immediate impact
  - Long-term: impact will be felt down the road
- Dilemmas
  - Players must weigh the consequences of their choices carefully, no optimal answer – something gained/lost either way
  - Can resonate emotionally with the player

## Improving Player Choices

- Rewards and punishment
  - Rewards are used more than punishment
  - Don't want to over-punish the player – stop playing
  - Threat of punishment creates tension (e.g., stealth mode)
  - Balanced system of rewards/punishment makes choices more interesting
  - Guidelines:
    - Rewards that are useful in obtaining victory carry greater weight
    - Rewards with a romantic association (magic/gold) appear to be more valuable
    - Rewards that are tied to the storyline have an added impact
  - Timing/quantity of rewards is important
    - A small stream of rewards can become meaningless
    - Larger, less frequent rewards or random rewards (beware gambling-like elements) are more effective

## Improving Player Choices

- Anticipation: more meaningful choices from allowing players to clearly see/anticipate consequences of their actions (e.g., Chess)
  - Incomplete info (e.g., fog of war) can also build anticipation
- Surprise: can reinvest players in the game, balance between randomness of surprise and making player choices meaningful
- Progress: satisfying to see choices made result in progress
  - Design milestones for the player – small goals along the way
    - Advertise them to the player and reward them for completing
    - Mini-arcs of gameplay (~1hr) with memorable moments
- The end: satisfaction of defeating other players or working together to defeat the game, resolving the story, final cutscene

## \#4 The Lens of Surprise

- Surprise is the root of humour, strategy, problem solving
- What will surprise players when they play my game?
- Does the story/rules/art/tech in my game have surprises?
- Do the rules give players ways to surprise each other/themselves?

### \#6 The Lens of Curiosity

- Think about the player's motivations, not just the game goals
- What questions does my game put into the player's mind?
- What am I doing to make them care about these questions?
- What can I do to make them invent even more questions?

## \#7 The Lens of Endogenous Value

- The player's willingness to pretend the game is important
- What is valuable to the players in my game?
- How can I make it more valuable to them?
- What is the relationship between value the game and the player's motivations?

### \#8 The Lens of Problem Solving

- What problems does my game as the player to solve?
- Are there hidden problems to solve that arise as part of gameplay?
- How can my game generate new problems so that players keep coming back?

## \#12 The Lens of Resonance

- What is it about my game that feels powerful and special?
- When I describe my game to people, what ideas get them really excited?

## Taxonomy of Player Types/Pleasures

- Competitor: plays to best other players, regardless of the game
- Explorer: curious about the world, seeks outside boundaries
- Collector: items, trophies, knowledge, creates sets
- Achiever: plays for achievement, ladders and levels
- Joker: plays for the fun of playing, social, can be annoying
- Artist: driven by creativity, creation, design
- Director: loves to be in charge, direct the play
- Storyteller: loves to create/live in worlds of fantasy/imagination
- Performer: loves to put on a show for others
- Craftsman: wants to build, craft, engineer, puzzle things out

- Different players enjoy different things and have different styles of play

## LeBlanc's Taxonomy of Game Pleasures

- Sensation: using your senses, game aesthetics
- Fantasy: imaginary world, imagining yourself differently
- Narrative: dramatic unfolding of sequence of events
- Challenge: core pleasure of gameplay, enough for some players
- Fellowship: friendship, cooperation, community, key for some
- Discovery: seeking/finding new things, secrets, strategy
- Expression: creating, building, sharing, customising, expressing
- Submission: entering the magic circle

## Bartle's Taxonomy of Player Types

- Achievers: want to achieve game goals, challenge
- Explorers: want to explore the game, discovery
- Socialisers: relationships with others, fellowship

## Additional Pleasures

- Anticipation: waiting for a pleasure you know is coming
- Completion: collecting all the treasure, clearing the level
- Delight in another's misfortune: schadenfreude, MP games
- Gift giving: making someone else happy with a surprise gift
- Humour: two unconnected things united by paradigm shift
- Possibility: having many options to choose from
- Pride in an accomplishment: can persist long after
- Surprise: pleasure from being surprised
- Thrill: "fear minus death equals fun" - roller coaster
- Triumph over adversity: accomplishing when pushed to limits
- Wonder: overwhelming feeling of awe and amazement

## Player Traits Model

- Aesthetic orientation: enjoy exploring the world, observing scenery, appreciate graphics, sound, art style; low = focus more on gameplay
- Narrative orientation: enjoy complex stories/narratives; low = prefer less story, skip cutscenes
- Goal orientation: enjoy completing goals/quests/tasks, complete 100%, explore all options, complete all collections; low = leave optional quests unfinished, relaxed if don't complete 100%
- Social orientation: prefer to play together with others online or in the same space and enjoy multiplayer and competitive gaming communities; low = prefer single player
- Challenge orientation: prefer difficult games, hard challenges, fast-paced  gameplay; low = prefer easier or casual games
