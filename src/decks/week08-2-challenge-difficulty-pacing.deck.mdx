## Why we need challenge

- Challenge measures our strength of character, or tests our skills
  - Positive and negative challenges
  - Empowerment and reward for meeting a challenge
- Challenges test player skills; enjoyment comes from:
  - Learning to use the gameplay mechanics
  - New, enjoyable ways to test your ability

## Challenge is not a goal

- Challenge is not a desirable goal in itself – inserting extra challenges does not increase the value of the experience
- If there is no reason for the challenge, the player:
  - Suffers desensitisation and boredom
  - Loses motivation and builds up resentment
  - Stops taking it seriously
  - Stops feeling an emotional connection
- Without context, challenge is just hardship
  - Serve a higher goal – why am I doing this? – and allow a positive outcome – what will I get?

## What difficulty is

- Challenge is a formal invitation or demand to overcome adversity
- Difficulty is how well the player is equipped for it
  - Challenge relative to player skill
- The same challenge differs in difficulty per player
  - Humans have wildly different abilities and skills
  - Suitable for one, insurmountable for another

## Misjudging the player

- Misjudging the abilities of the player:
  - Can make a level too easy or too hard
  - Can lead to boredom or frustration
  - Can severely damage a player’s enjoyment of a game
- Frustration can lead players to quit the game
- Avoiding boredom is not just a matter of increasing difficulty
  - An activity that requires no new skills, and does not refine current skill sets, becomes less interesting and less rewarding

- Challenge can test players, affect pacing, or let players measure their own strength

- Players might choose a difficulty level – ridiculously hard

## Dynamic difficulty adjustment

- The game reacts to the apparent skill level of the player and adjusts difficulty accordingly
- The experience is tailored to the varying skill levels and play styles of its players
- Example – adjust AI based on player deaths
  - Player dies too many times in a conflict area – adjust difficulty downwards, making AI shooting less accurate
- Many ways to implement it
- Good results if it is not overly obvious to the player

## Data cannot read emotions

- Performance data gives limited info for important decisions
  - What if the player enjoys a playing style that leaves them close to death most of the time?
  - What if the player enjoys shooting the scenery as well as shooting enemies?
- We cannot gather data that objectively tells us the player’s real emotions and experiences
  - Although there is active research on this topic
  - Reactive/passive difficulty adjustment will always be based on subjective guesses
  - Interpretation can yield wrong conclusions and bad adjustments

## Difficulty tips

- Challenge is a tool for something specific, not a goal in itself
- Arbitrary difficulty – pointless challenge
  - Takes an existing game mechanic and makes it awkward for no reason
- Prohibitive difficulty – some games are too hard to enjoy
  - Designers play so much that they reach an atypical skill level and entertain themselves
  - Entertain your audience, not yourself
- Unfair challenges
  - Some designers think of the player as their enemy and try to kill them as harshly as possible

## Unfair ways to challenge

- Gameplay can test a player’s skills, but should not present tasks or obstacles unfairly:
  - Instant death, unannounced
  - Something extremely hard without training the player first
  - Obscure nonsensical puzzles
  - Extended memory-based gameplay
  - Overly harsh restrictions – impossibly short time trials
  - Lack of save points
  - One-way doors that close behind the player without warning

## Pacing and the flow channel

- Exert control over the timing and intensity of the gameplay experience
- Pacing should keep players in “the zone” – the flow channel
  - The perfect state where immersion and enjoyment keep players in gaming bliss
  - As a level progresses, maintain the balance between challenge and ability, along a learning curve that develops over time

## Designing for game pacing

- Maintain a sensible learning curve
- Under-challenge – the player becomes bored, but can still try to entertain themselves
- Over-challenge – the player becomes frustrated and may stop playing

<div class="image-credit">Kremers – Ch. 12, 16</div>

## Pacing: linear progression

- Linear progression – a straight path from A to B
  - Effective if it is not overused
  - Great clarity: progression along one path
  - Enjoyable when players must finish an obstacle course to progress
  - Pacing is controlled by the gameplay along the way
  - Easy to create and test such a formal environment

  - Can show the player their goal from the start – enhances the context in which players act

## Pacing: sandbox

- Sandbox – players come up with their own solutions, or at least the illusion that they can
  - The world does not feel too prescribed
  - Raw building material to make the player’s own mark on the world, bend it to their will
- Not always easy to influence pacing in such an environment
  - Place conditional events in the world, create natural-looking bottlenecks
  - The player must be of a particular strength to access an area; a monster is only released once the player has a certain weapon

## Pacing: stickiness

- Stickiness – slow the player down with areas that are sticky
- Positive reasons for staying in an area:
  - A safe area with some fun activity
  - RPG selling, crafting and buying
  - A tempting discrete challenge that may yield a great reward
- If stickiness is optional, the player never feels resentful about this manipulation
- Half-Life 2 – many optional areas, filled with opportunities for almost carefree play

## Pacing: push and pull

- Elements that influence progress, forcibly or by necessity
- Push – players go down a lift
  - It breaks: no backtracking
- Pull – chased by a strong enemy
  - A health pack visible ahead
- Pacing adjustments by pushing and pulling
  - Natural if done well

## Pacing: space

- Pacing is as much determined by what you leave out as what you put in
- Game design needs spaces where nothing happens
  - Spaces between gameplay events are part of the gameplay experience as a whole
  - Sometimes leaving empty space is a game design event in its own right
- Room for the player to breathe, reflect, think, observe, contemplate and strategise
  - As vital as the action-packed moments in the game

## Pacing: other methods

- Puzzles as pacing devices – the likely duration of a puzzle sequence gives some control over pacing
- Fear – scare players into adjusting their pace of progression
- Hurry – create a necessity to rush
- Time – affect the progression of time to create pacing changes
- Physics – let the properties of the environment determine progress
- AI – let the player depend on the progression of an NPC
