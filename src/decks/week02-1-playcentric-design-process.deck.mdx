## Player experience goals

- Keep the player experience in mind at every phase, and test with target players
- The first way to bring the player into the design
- Goals for the type of experience the player will have, not features
- Not how those goals will be implemented
- Features are brainstormed later to meet the goals, then playtested against them

## Beyond the feature list

- Start at a high level: what is interesting and engaging about your game?
- What experiences will players describe to their friends?
- Going beyond features is difficult
- What are players thinking when they make choices? How are they feeling?
- Are the choices offered rich and interesting?

<div class="image-credit">Fullerton p.12</div>

## Example experience goals

- “Players will have to cooperate to win, but the game will be structured so that they can never trust each other”
- “Players will have the freedom to pursue the goals of the game in any order they choose”
- “Players will feel a sense of happiness and playfulness rather than competitiveness”

## The Lens of Emotion (lens 1)

- What emotions would I like my player to experience? Why?
- What emotions are players having when they play? Why?
- How can I bridge the gap between emotions players are having and the emotions I'd like them to have?

## Prototyping and playtesting

- Prototype and playtest ideas early
- A playable version straight after brainstorming
- Physical prototype of core gameplay
- Paper, pen, cards, acted out
  - Played by the designer and friends
- Perfect the simple model before any development
- Instant feedback on experience goals

## Flow of the iterative process

- Player experience goals are set
- An idea or system is conceived
- Formalised — written down or prototyped
- Tested against player experience goals — playtested or exhibited for feedback

<div class="image-credit">Fullerton p.16</div>


---

## Reading the results

- Negative results and a fundamentally flawed idea: go back to the first step
- Results point to improvements: modify and test again
- Positive results and a successful system: the iterative process is complete

<div class="image-credit">Fullerton p.16</div>

## Step 1: Brainstorming

- Set player experience goals
- Concepts or mechanics that might achieve them
- Narrow the list down to the top three
- A one-page description of each — a concept document
- Test your written concepts with potential players
  - Rough visual mock-ups help communicate ideas

- Digital prototyping is discussed in chapter 8

## Step 5: Design documentation

- Prototyping leaves you notes and ideas for the “real” game
  - Use them to develop a full list of goals for the game
- Most developers don't create large static documents
  - Smaller, online, as-needed documentation
  - Suits the flexible, collaborative nature of modern design
- Documentation is a collaboration tool that changes and grows with production

## Step 6: Production

- Work with the team so goals are achievable and priorities agreed
- Staff up with a full team and plan development “sprints”
- Evaluate the game as a team after each sprint to keep on track
- Don't lose sight of the playcentric process during production
- Problems and changes should get smaller throughout — major issues were resolved during prototyping
- Designing your game during production costs time, money and frustration

## Playtesting at every stage

- Playtest at every stage of development
- You can't advocate for the player if you don't know what they are thinking
- Playtesting is the best way to elicit feedback and gain insight
- Continually isolate and playtest every aspect of your game

<div class="image-credit">Fullerton p.21</div>

## Rule of the loop

- The more times you test and improve your design, the better your game will be
- Related to Agile — later in the course
- Informal loop: think of an idea, try it out, keep changing and testing it until it seems good enough

## The formal loop

- State the problem
- Brainstorm some possible solutions
- Choose a solution

<div class="image-credit">Schell p.100, 114-115</div>


---

## The formal loop: prototypes

- List the risks of using that solution
- Build prototypes to mitigate the risks
- Test the prototypes — if they are good enough, stop
- State new problems you are trying to solve — go to 2

<div class="image-credit">Schell p.100, 114-115</div>

## Opportunities for innovation

- Unique play mechanics, or new genres
- Appealing to new players, not just “gamers”
- New platforms — gestural, AR/VR
- Games that integrate into real-world spaces
  - Alternate reality games, location-based games

<div class="image-credit">Fullerton p.23</div>


---

## Innovation: harder problems

- Solving difficult problems in game design
  - Integrating story and gameplay, creating empathy, emotionally rich games, games and learning
- Impactful games — culturally, individually
- Innovation comes from long-term experimentation
