## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- What is a Level?
- Levels are distinct areas in a game: constrained/separated by geographical area, content, or gameplay
- Many older games had one level: each “level” was a variation in gameplay (e.g., Space Invaders, Centipede) or simple variations of one level (e.g., Pac-Man)
- Real-time strategy – a map and set of objectives
- Racing – a track
- Sports – stadium/other team, aesthetic
- Simulation – no levels, player constructs play, entire game one level

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Level Separation
- How gameplay is broken into levels has a big impact on the flow of a game
  - Players often play one level at a time, or put off stopping “just until I finish this level”
  - Save points can be limited to after a level
  - Player feels accomplishment after a level
- A level is like a book chapter / act in play: levels have tension/release moments (dramatic curve)

- Level Order
- Important to overall flow/pacing of the game (e.g., order of different types of levels, bosses)
- Story-centric games (e.g., BioShock) need lots of planning – levels must support story (events, environment, enemies)
- No story constraints in some games (e.g., Doom) – earlier levels easier, restricted creatures

- Components of a Level
- Design of levels is core to gameplay: “where the rubber hits the road”
- Goal of each level – provide an engaging gameplay experience
- Keep in mind the focus of the game:
- What is the game trying to accomplish?

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Components of a Level: Puzzles
- Progressing through a level may involve more than finding a path while fighting
- Might involve figuring out what to do to open a door or remove an obstacle (e.g., “switch flipping” or placing explosives in FPS games)

- More sophisticated variants on switch flipping
- Player must figure something out to progress
- Understanding game physics to solve a physics puzzle
- Not just finding, but finding and figuring it out
- Puzzles need to enhance gaming experience
- Not just delay gameplay or pad out game
- Important to determine emphasis on puzzles
- What is the role/importance of puzzles in this game?

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Components of a Level: Exploration
- Important in adventure, open-world, RPGs (Fallout, Grand Theft Auto)
- Not just a bridge between action – thrill of exploring a new world
- Exciting exploration is not just architecture – finding new things is a reward for exploration

- Think about how the player will explore the level
- What cool piece of art will they see around the next corner?
- How excited will players be to find new areas?
- Playtesting with fresh eyes is key
- Flow of the level is central to exploration:
- Will there be one way to proceed or several paths to explore?
- Will the way to proceed be obvious or need to be found?
- Action games lead players directly to next conflict
- Exploration games make paths less obvious

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Components of a Level: Storytelling
- Levels are vital for setting and storytelling
- Levels need to support the story
  - Appearance of characters and story goals
  - Level goals should be tied directly to the progression of the story in story-driven games

- Story goals must be known prior to construction of a level
- Level settings must be chosen to support story
- Story must be loose enough to allow the level designer to be creative in making the level
- Gameplay concerns
- Need to balance strategy, action, puzzles, exploration
- Cannot balance before level exists – story shouldn’t be overly restrictive
- Story might need to change to accommodate gameplay needs of the level

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Components of a Level: Action
- Most games contain action (fighting, running, driving, jumping)
  - How much action and at what pace?
  - How much of the level should be action-filled?
  - How many battles will the player fight?
  - Is the action fast and furious?
  - Are there breaks between conflicts?
  - Is there a constant fear of death?

- Designer needs to consider:
- How the enemy AI functions
- How the map supports conflicts
- What geometry will give players lots of locations to duck / cover while dodging enemy fire
- How the level can be setup to allow the player to figure out their own strategy for winning

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Level Flow: Action/Adventure
- Player plays a level from distinct beginning to end
- Exploring the space is central, significantly less fun to play more than once
- Encounters with characters/adversaries carefully predetermined and set up
- Gameplay is identical each time, flow is linear with maybe a few choices of how to get from A to B

- Level Flow: Role-Playing Games
- Similar to action with a bit less linearity
- Designer intends for player to navigate a location in a particular way
- Players can usually choose order in which different actions are performed
- “hub” style gameplay allows players to branch off on different adventures while returning to a central location (e.g., town), players pick challenges to hone their skills

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Level Flow: Strategy
- Action is less “canned”, battles can take place anywhere, different locations provide specific strategic advantages, battles can start in one location and move to another
- Exploration not a central part, sections of the map can go unexplored and unexploited
- Gameplay is less predictable, flow is less defined

- Level Flow: First-Person Shooter Deathmatch
- Death-match close to flow of strategy games, exploration less important, combat can take place anywhere
- Players like to stick to one map for a while, players need to have explored the map thoroughly to win
- Exploration / memorisation are integral parts of the meta-game (exploration is a means to an end, not an end in itself)

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Level Flow: Sports
- Non-linear flow, resembles death match/strategy levels
- Action takes place all over the court
- Players’ movement flows back and forth across the level, covering the same ground but in unique and unpredictable ways
- No exploration as whole court is always visible

- Level Flow: Racing
- Distinct start/end locations, similar to exploration-oriented action games, but start/end is the same (loop)
- Race-path repeated multiple times, non-linear in terms of gameplay and how players race their opponents
- Modern racing games incorporate some exploration elements of action games by making levels visually stunning and varied
- Alternate paths/shortcuts for varied gameplay (e.g., SSX)

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Good Level Design: Progression
- Players cannot get stuck (progression blockers): can’t block path, get trapped, kill vital character, or simply too hard to progress
  - Good planning and lots of playtesting, multiple ways to progress
- Success the first time?
  - Too easy if most players can win the first time, possibility should exist, player should feel they have a fair chance

- Good Level Design: Navigating
- Landmarks: memorable landmarks to ease exploration (e.g., ornate room, large statue, pool of lava)
- Limited backtracking: don’t make the player backtrack large sections of the map, have branching paths to lead back
- Navigable areas clearly marked: know where can/can’t go by looking at environment (e.g., lighting, textures, obstacles)

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Good Level Design: Goals
- Critical path: primary goal of level (e.g., head north, kill creature), gives player a sense of direction
- Sub-goals: various tasks contribute to final goal, feedback/rewards, getting closer to victory

- Good Level Design: Choices
- Choice on how to accomplish goals
- FPS – bonus items, ways to kill the enemies
- Strategy – different places to battle, rally, gather resources
- Adventure – multiple solutions to puzzles, different characters
- Choices should be real choices, not:
- Both options lead to same outcome
- One choice is clearly superior

## Theory: Level Design Rudolf Kremers. (2009) Level Design: Concept, Theory, and Practice. Richard Rouse III (2005) Game Design: Theory & Practice, 2nd Ed. Ch. 23. (cont.)

- Process of Level Design
- 1. Preliminary
  - Which gameplay/mechanics are close to final?
  - Which gameplay/mechanics are incomplete?
  - How much will the game change?
  - How will future changes impact your level?
  - Levels and gameplay will be designed in tandem – both will evolve over the course of development

- What does the level need to do from a gameplay/story perspective?
- Challenges, environments, pace, rewards, story elements?
- General sketch of layout when know what level needs to accomplish
- Changes to a sketch are easier than a fully constructed level
- Use sketch to get feedback
