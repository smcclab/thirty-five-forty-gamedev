## Introduction to Playtesting

- The single most important activity a designer engages in
  - Often the least understood and left to last
- Misconception: playtesting is just playing the game and giving feedback
- Reality: full process that includes selection, recruiting, preparation, controls, analysis
- Internal design review: game developers play the game and discuss the features
- Quality assurance: testing and searching for flaws/bugs
- Focus group testing: marketing bring in people to play a game and ask about marketing questions (price, selling points)
- Usability testing: systematically analyse how users interact with your software by recording their mouse movements, eye movements, navigation patterns, etc

## Introduction to Playtesting

- Numerous ways to conduct playtesting
  - Informal and qualitative (e.g., 10 friends in your garage)
  - Structured and quantitative (e.g., 100s in a professional lab)
- Goal: gain useful feedback from players to improve your game
  - Make sure your game is:
    - Functioning as intended
    - Internally complete
    - Balanced and fun to play

## Playtesting and Iterative Design

- Designer is the player's advocate throughout the development process
- Continual iterative process
  - Cycles get tighter as production moves forward
  - Smaller and smaller issues to solve and changes to make
  - No fundamental or dramatic changes towards end
- Playtesting in beta (fully functional game) is too late
  - No fundamental changes, stuck with core gameplay
- Playtest and iterate from the start

## Types of Playtesters

- Self-testing (you!) - individual or team
  - Early stages of development and throughout project
  - Experiment with fundamental concepts, core mechanics
  - Fix obvious problems
  - Try playing with a "fresh" mind
- Confidants - people you know well (outside design team)
  - Bring fresh eyes, but they are not objective
  - Incomplete prototype – might need to explain things
  - Aim to play prototype without much intervention
  - Physical prototype – write down the full set of rules
  - Digital prototype – interface in place, some written rules
  - Wean yourself from confidents when you have a playable prototype – to get more and more balanced feedback

<aside class="slide-aside">

- You will naturally try out your game repeatedly to see how it functions.
- You will continue to self-test throughout the process, but you will need to rely on others more as you go.

</aside>

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.9 p.277-310</div>

```comment
self-testing – early stage of development when you’re experimenting with fundamental concepts, individually or as a group, come up with core mechanics, fix obvious problems with play experience – goal is to make the game work (rough approximation of final product), self-test throughout project
playtesting with confidants – after foundation stage and prototype is playable, test with people you know well (outside design team), might need to be present/explain things (incomplete prototype), aim to have a prototype that can be played without intervention (complete interface, maybe some written rules), do not rely on these people (not objective)
```

## Types of Playtesters

- People you don’t know
  - Difficult to let strangers play your incomplete game
  - Fresh perspective
  - Nothing to lose/gain by telling you how they feel
  - No knowledge of game
- People who represent your target audience
  - Who spend money on games like yours
  - More relevant feedback
    - Compare to other games
    - Know what they like/dislike (and vocal about it)
  - Target audience can provide insight no one else can
  - More diverse group the better (within target audience)

```comment
playtesting with people you don’t know – difficult to let strangers play incomplete game, need their fresh perspective (nothing to lose/gain by telling you how they honestly feel), no knowledge of the game

```

## Types of Playtesters

- Who should be there? (depends on why)
  - Developers (convenient, know how to play the game, but too close to the game and biased)
  - Friends (available, comfortable, biased, not honest)
  - Expert players in your genre (detailed feedback, comparison to similar games, not representative of most players)
  - New players (fresh eyes, usability/communication, initial appeal, but also want to test long-term play)

## Types of Playtesters

- Ideal playtesters
  - Advertise for testers
    - Recruit from a range of sources
  - Screen applicants
    - Want articulate people to convey opinions (can they hold a conversation over the phone?)
    - Basic demographics (hobbies, why did they apply, how often do they buy these games?)
- This is not relevant for your projects
  - You can make use of other students in your workshop
  - Students in other workshops will peer assess your game, do not get them to playtest it

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.9 p.277-310</div>

```comment
finding the ideal playtesters – tap into community (recruit from high school, college, sports club, social organisations etc), post online or put ad in the paper (more sources give better candidate pool) – then screen and turn down applicants (once you have enough applicants), look for tests articulate enough to convey opinions (e.g. can hold a decent conversation on the phone), ask questions for basic demographics – hobbies, why did you apply, how often do you buy this type of game (if not a consumer of this type of game then feedback is less useful)
```

## Playtesting Prototype Phases


### Completeness

- An internally complete game:
  - The players can operate the game without reaching any point at which either the gameplay or the functionality is compromised
- You can say your game is complete at any stage
  - Also, no game is ever complete
- Gaps in the rules
  - Result in an exploit, dead-end or complete breakdown
  - “the rules don’t say either way”, “I’m completely stuck”, “you can’t do that”
- Go back to the rules and look for holes

```comment
Completeness – an internally complete game is one in which the players can operate the game without reaching any point at which either the gameplay or the functionality is compromised
```

## Playtesting Prototype Phases


### Completeness – Loopholes (exploits)

- A flaw in the system that users can exploit to gain an unfair or unintended advantage
  - Ruins the experience for all players
- Players ferret them out, can take over main gameplay
- Loopholes vs. features
  - Do they improve the experience, or ruin it for some players?
- Won’t find them all (particularly for complex games)
  - Too many possibilities
  - Public beta test
  - Use control situations to test aspects in isolation
  - Find people who enjoy this
  - Instruct players to disrupt the system

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.10 p.311-320</div>

```comment
figure omitted pending copyright review (see Fullerton Ch.10 p.311-320)
```

```comment
Loopholes – a flaw in the system which users can exploit to gain an unfair or unintended advantage – in videogames so many possibilities difficult to test them all, some players make a point of ferreting them out
Loopholes versus features – system issues can be loopholes or benefits
Usually won’t find all loopholes before release, some developers use a public beta test – can dampen sales of the game but can be a valuable tool
When discover a loophole, need to tweak system and perform another playtest – iterative process and each loophole can take days/weeks to solve
To find and remove loopholes:
Use control situations to test aspects of the system in isolation, do a series of playtests where you instruct testers to attempt to disrupt the system – challenge them to see who can come up with the most creative way to get ahead, find testers who enjoy figuring out alternative or subversive solutions
```

## Playtesting Prototype Phases


### Completeness – Dead Ends

- Player gets stuck
  - Cannot continue towards the game objective no matter what they do
  - Adventure games are susceptible (e.g., if need to collect objects to use later)
  - Player runs out of resources in strategy
  - Player stumbles into a space and cannot get out in a FPS

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.10 p.311-320</div>

```comment

```

```comment
Dead-ends – occurs when a player gets stranded in the game and cannot continue towards the game objective no matter what they do (adventure games susceptible to this – if need to collect objects and use them to solve a puzzle; strategy games where the player cannot win as they run out of resources; FPS - space player stumbles into and cannot get out of)
```

## Playtesting Prototype Phases

- Balance (Week 4 Theory)
- Process of making sure the game meets the goals you’ve set for the player experience
- The system is of the scope and complexity you envisioned
- The elements are working together without undesired results
- Fun (Week 3 Theory)
- Is your game fun?
- Is your game accessible? (to new players)
- Can someone pick up and play the game?
- Need to test people who are:
  - Part of the target audience, Objective (not friends), Have never played the game
