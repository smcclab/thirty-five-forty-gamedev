## Conducting a Playtesting Session

- Your role is as an investigator/observer, not a designer
- You must give playtesters access to the game, lead them through a useful playtest, record what they do/say, analyse their responses
- Don't tell them what to think about it or explain how it works
- Let them play it with minimal explanation and make mistakes
- See how each person approaches it
- Ideally, have an objective person run the tests while you watch from a distance (behind glass or via video)
- Create a test script to keep you on track and remind you of your role as observer

## Conducting a Playtesting Session

- Introduction (2-3 mins)
  - Welcome playtesters, thank them for participating, introduce yourself, brief explanation of playtesting process, let them know if you are video/audio recording (and that it is just for your reference, not for sharing), intro anyone else present
- Warm-up discussion (5 mins)
  - Write some questions to help them get talking, such as:
    - What are some of the games you play?
    - What do you like about these games?
    - What was the last game you purchased?

## Conducting a Playtesting Session

- Discussion of game experience (15-20 mins)
  - Have a 1-on-1 discussion after play session
  - Probe for overall appeal, interest, challenge, understanding
  - Example questions:
    - Overall, what were your thoughts about the game?
    - What were your thoughts about the game play?
    - Were you able to learn quickly how to play?
    - What is the objective of the game?
    - How would you describe the game to someone else?
    - Is there anything you wish you knew before starting to play?
    - Is there anything you didn’t like about the game?
    - Was there anything you found confusing?
  - You will have specific questions/focus based on your goals
  - Focus on the most important questions you have at this stage

## Conducting a Playtesting Session

- Wrap-up
  - Thank the playtesters
  - You could give them a token gift
- Tips
  - Listen to their feedback without responding / defending
  - Write down any criticisms, don’t respond to them
  - Remember you are looking for things to improve, not praise
  - It can be hard to hear criticism of your creation
  - Do not lead your playtesters to say what you want to hear
  - Better to hear the bad news now, then after you release
  - People in groups will sway each other (group think) and some people will dominate (group dynamics)

## Methods of Playtesting

- Individual vs. Group: group dynamics good for idea generation but bad for evaluation
- One-on-one testing: observe as they play, take notes, ask questions
- Group testing: get group to play together, observe the group and ask questions along the way
- Feedback forms: give each person a standard list of questions after playing, compare results (good for quantitative results, identifying frequency of problems)
- Interview: face-to-face, in-depth, verbal interview after playing
- Open discussion: individual/group after playing, take notes, open or structured
- Metrics: collect automatically in-game or via other tools
  - What features are players using/not using, used for tuning
- Combine approaches: fit to game, space, time and money
  - Not testing is not an option!

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.9 p.277-310</div>

```comment
Usability testing takes place individually, group dynamics are good for generating ideas but bad for evaluating
One-on-one testing – sit down with individuals and watch over their shoulders as they play, take notes and ask them questions along the way
Group testing – get a group of people and allow them to play the game together, observe the group and ask questions as they play
```

## A Primer for Playtesting

- Playtest before you think you are ready
- Strategise for early playtesting
- Know why you are playtesting (what question are you answering?)
- Prepare variations
- Be grateful to your playtesters (say thanks)
- Design the learning experience (and playtest)
- Blame yourself, not your playtesters
- Know your testers (e.g., hardcore gamer vs new player)
- Don't explain (explain as little as possible)
- Take notes (shows you are listening / care, prepare a notes sheet)
- Be selfish (purpose is for you to learn, not for testers to have fun)
- Encourage your playtesters to talk aloud (and remind them)

## A Primer for Playtesting

- Notice everything (what they did, chose, how long it took)
- Shut up (don’t interfere, explain, tell them what to do)
- See the big picture (what are the human reactions?)
- Don’t be afraid of data (tracking the right data will help you)
- Answer a question with a question (how do you think it works?)
- Hunger for failure (look for what is not working)
- Discuss what happened (focus on most important aspects)
- Put feedback into context (players can help identify issues, not fix)
- Collaborate with your playtesters (gather their fresh ideas)
- The cruelly honest playtest (the truth hurts)
- Embrace the unexpected (it might be better than the plan)
- The playtest's the thing (get the process right)
- Break these rules

## Taking Notes

- Can’t remember all the comments / feedback from players
  - Most likely to remember what you wanted/expected to hear
- Write down - date, feedback from testers, own observations
- Use a form to capture observations and comments
  - Example on next slide with 3 parts:
    - In-game observations (your thoughts as you watch them play)
    - Post-game questions (help elicit opinions on key aspects)
    - Revision ideas (your ideas for making the game better)
  - Should be used alongside a script
  - Not all questions will be relevant – tailor to your needs
  - Write down more questions than you plan to use and then rank them in order of importance
  - Don't overwhelm playtesters with questions, go for quality/focus

```comment
Imperative to take notes, can’t remember all the comments/feedback from players
```

## Usability Techniques

- Do not lead
  - Quietly observe
  - If they ask questions – ask what they think they should do
  - If cannot proceed – problem identified
- Remind playtesters to think aloud
  - Can see where problems occur by observing, but not why
  - Ask them to explain what they’re thinking
  - Show problems you didn’t know existed
  - Need to prompt most people
- Quantitative data
  - Use feedback forms to generate data that shows trends
  - Can use prioritise severity of issues
- Metrics – measuring what people do or report
  - Case studies p.303-306

## Data Gathering (Quantitative)

- Results in lots of numbers
  - Need to know how to analyse and what to do with them
- Have clearly defined objectives
  - Before you measure something - what do you want to know?
- Use in conjunction with qualitative data
  - Why did they give a particular rating?
  - Why did it take them so long to solve a puzzle?
- Examples
  - Time it takes to read rules
  - Number of clicks to perform a function
  - Heat maps of where players have explored in a level
  - Track speed players advance in a level
  - Ask testers to rank ease of use of certain features on a scale of one to ten
  - Ask testers to choose between options to see which features are more important
  - Physiological signals (e.g., heart rate, galvanic skin response, eye tracking)

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.9 p.277-310</div>

```comment
Quantitative – record the time it takes to read rules, count the number of clicks to perform a certain function, track the speed at which players advance in a level, ask testers to rank ease of use of certain features on a scale of one to ten, choose between certain options to see what features are most important to them
Quantitative measurements give rise to lots of numbers, need to know how to analyse them / what to do with them – have clearly defined objectives before you start, before you measure something – what is it that you want to know?
Use in conjunction with qualitative
```

## Control Situations

- Test a specific portion of the game mechanics
  - End of game, random event that rarely occurs, special situation, particular level, new features
- Early: test basic functionality without balance / fairness
- Later: test for exploits or dead-ends, focus on accessibility of interface or balance
- Can repeatedly experience an event under a variety of conditions
  - Don’t need to start from the beginning
  - Start at any point
  - Control conditions, set variables
  - Test to see what happens under different conditions

<div class="image-credit">Playtesting — Fullerton Ch.9 p.277-310</div>

```comment
Force players to test a specific portion of the game mechanics – e.g. end of the game, a random event that rarely takes place, special situation within the game, a particular level of a game, new features
Early – test basic functionality without balance/fairness, later – test for loopholes/dead-ends, focus sessions on accessibility of interface or navigation system
Allows testers to repeatedly experience an event under a variety of conditions
Don’t have to start from the beginning and play all the way through – start at any point, control conditions, set variables – test to see what happens under different conditions
```
