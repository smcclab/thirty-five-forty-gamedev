## Communication {#communication}

Course announcements, materials and discussion were run through Wattle in 2024,
with separate forums for Unity support, general and assessment questions, and
research participation.

In 2024 the policy was explicit: ask questions on the forums, not by email.
Questions emailed to the convener or tutors were redirected to the forum. Email
was reserved for personal matters. Extension requests went through the

## Attendance and participation {#attendance}

The workshops are the timetabled contact and they are where the design work
happens. The prototype assessments ask you to document workshop activities week
by week and to supply evidence for each one, so missing workshops costs you
marks in the reports even though attendance is not marked directly.

The same is true of pace. The assessments are built on evidence collected
across the semester --- sketches, photographs of paper prototypes, screenshots,
build history. Work started shortly before a due date cannot produce that
evidence.

## Laptop requirement {#laptop}

You need a laptop that can run the Unity editor and that you can bring to every
workshop. Workshops mix physical prototyping with work in the engine, and
playtesting sessions in the second half of semester are run on your own
machines.

## Platform and format restrictions {#restrictions}

Every prototype and the final game were subject to the same restrictions in
2024, and they shape what you can design:

- **Single player only.** The game must be playable by one player.
- **Computer game.** It must be playable on a PC or Mac with a keyboard and
  mouse.
- **Unity Play.** It must be published as a WebGL build to Unity Play.
- **Fifteen minutes.** The final game is played by peer markers for exactly
  fifteen minutes --- no more for a long game, and repeated play for a short
  one.

Design for those constraints from the start. A game that needs a controller, a
second player or an hour to get going will be marked badly through no fault of
its craft.

## Asset policy {#assets}

The asset rules differ between the individual and group assessments, and the
penalties are severe, so read them carefully.

| Assessment | Assets permitted | Penalty |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Prototype Progress](/assessments/progress/) | Primitives only. No assets at all, including free, paid and self-made. Materials are allowed; textures are not. | 50% of the prototype mark |
| [Game Proposal and Prototype](/assessments/proposal/) | Primitives only, as above. | 50% of the prototype mark |
| [Game Project](/assessments/project/) | Free assets from the Unity Asset Store, the Unity Course Library and approved repositories; graphics, sound and animation only, not code or functionality. Paid assets are not permitted, including ones obtained free through gifts or sales. | Mark of 0 for undeclared assets; see the assessment spec |

The reason for the primitives-only rule on the individual prototypes is that
the marks go to gameplay. A grey box that plays well beats a beautiful scene
that does not.

All assets used in the group project must be declared and acknowledged both in
your Unity project and on the project coversheet. A list of approved asset
repositories was maintained on the course forum.

## Group work and peer assessment {#groups}

The [Game Project](/assessments/project/) is done in small teams formed part
way through semester. In 2024, teams were drawn from within a single workshop
group; you could choose your own team or be allocated to one, and you had to
have submitted the proposal to be eligible to join a team. Team size was set
from enrolment numbers.

The project is marked by peer playtesting: each team plays and rates two or
three other teams' games through a structured survey. Completing your peer
assessment on time and to a satisfactory standard is a condition of receiving a
mark for the project at all --- so is submitting the coversheet. In 2024 a
missing coversheet or peer assessment meant an individual mark of 0 for the
game.

Your individual contribution is evidenced separately, through the
[developer diary](/assessments/diary/). Work done jointly must be identified as
such, with your own part in it described.

## Research participation {#research}

A small component of the course requires participation in games or
human--computer interaction research, and a short written reflection on it. In
2024 this was 1.5--2 credits of participation in approved experiments through
[SONA](https://anupsych.sona-systems.com/), taken as course credit rather than
any other form of remuneration, and checked against SONA records.

Experiment availability is limited and bookings fill up. Start looking at the
beginning of semester; in 2024 extensions were not granted for experiment
availability. See [research participation](/assessments/research-reflection/).

## Academic integrity {#integrity}

All work you submit must be your own, or your team's, with any use of other
people's assets, code, tutorials or ideas clearly attributed. Code you follow
along with in the Unity Learn tutorials is expected and appropriate; presenting
a tutorial project as your own game design is not.

See the ANU
[academic integrity](https://www.anu.edu.au/students/academic-skills/academic-integrity)
pages for the university rules.

## Code of conduct {#code-of-conduct}

Everyone in this course is responsible for making it a place where people can
show unfinished work, play each other's games honestly, and give and take
criticism without it becoming personal. Playtesting only works if testers tell
you the truth and designers can hear it.

The ANU
[Code of Conduct](https://www.anu.edu.au/students/program-administration/program-management/anu-code-of-conduct-for-students)
applies to all students.
